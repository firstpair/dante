# Reading this edition

Open the FirstPair Reader from the ribbon, the command palette, or the link
at the top of `Home.md`. The Italian is always the left column; the English
stands beside it. The **English** checkbox at the top hides the translation
when you want to read the Italian alone.

Four English translations travel with this vault — Longfellow's line-for-line
verse (1867, the default), Cary's blank verse (1814), Norton's prose
(1891–92), and Sibbald's terza rima of the Inferno (1884) — and five Russian
ones: Min's verse in Dante's own metre (1855–1904, the default, in modern
spelling and in the spelling of its editions), Petrov's terza rima Inferno
(1887), Fedorov's Inferno (1898), Chuiko's prose Inferno (1894), and Minaev's
Purgatorio and Paradiso (1874–79). With both languages on, Russian stands in
the middle and English on the right. Each column header names the
translation you are reading and rotates among the language's translations;
**+** beside it opens a second column of the same language, so two
translations of the same tercet stand side by side. Translations that keep
Dante's line count (Longfellow, Sibbald, Min, Petrov, Fedorov) show the same
verses on every row; the others are cut at the same fractions of the canto
and are marked ≈ (approximate).

Each row is one stable tercet (with a shorter final row where a canto closes
with a single line). Alignment is structural by canto and verse position; it
does not assert word-for-word equivalence. On a phone held upright the Reader
stacks each tercet — Italian first, English indented beneath it — and returns
to columns when you turn the phone sideways; the layout button in the toolbar
overrides that choice. `Home.md` lists every canto as a link that opens it in
the Reader.

Select an Italian word to open it in the dictionary drawer. Inflected and
old forms are analysed to their modern headword — the entry names the grammar
of the form and, where Dante's spelling differs, how it was restored — with
English definitions from the English Wiktionary and FreeDict, and usage
examples from the Commedia itself. The reader does not invent a gloss when
the edition has no entry.

Both texts are public domain: Dante's Italian from Project Gutenberg ebook
1012 and Henry Wadsworth Longfellow's translation from ebook 1004. Their
license notices travel with the vault under `Sources/`.
